# AI as a Mirror: Rethinking Software Development in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

In a recent episode of "Software-Architektur im Stream," renowned software consultant Kevlin Henney explored a profound metaphor for understanding artificial intelligence's role in software development: AI as a mirror. Rather than simply accelerating our work, AI reflects both our capabilities and our fundamental blindspots as an industry.

Henney, an independent consultant, speaker, and author of the popular "97 Things" series, emphasizes that AI functions as a multiplier—amplifying whatever practices, good or bad, already exist within an organization. This distinction proves critical. Companies with strong architectural foundations, rigorous testing practices, and clear objectives benefit tremendously from AI tools. Conversely, organizations plagued by technical debt and dysfunctional processes find themselves multiplying their problems rather than their productivity.

**The Central Problem: Speed Over Quality**

One of Henney's most provocative insights concerns the prevalent organizational obsession with velocity. Rather than asking "How do we go faster?" organizations should ask "What's slowing us down?" Most development teams, he argues, spend roughly 80% of their time on "failure demand"—fixing bugs, managing technical debt, and working around architectural problems—rather than adding genuine value.

This dynamic becomes even more critical with AI adoption. By generating more code faster, teams inadvertently create more bugs and cognitive debt, necessitating additional maintenance work. The result resembles driving with one foot simultaneously on the accelerator and brake—creating heat and frustration while making minimal progress.

**Architecture and Understanding**

Henney stresses that classical software engineering principles remain as relevant as ever. Good architecture, loose coupling, high cohesion, and clear interfaces benefit both human developers and AI systems. Large monolithic methods confuse both humans and language models alike. Interestingly, the very constraints that help AI work effectively—small components, clear boundaries, well-named functions—mirror what experienced developers have advocated for decades.

**A Warning for the Next Generation**

Perhaps most importantly, Henney urges the next generation of developers not to become passive "meat proxies" for AI systems. They must develop genuine understanding of code, akin to learning Latin—reading and comprehending existing systems deeply, not merely generating new ones. This knowledge directly enables better decision-making and more effective use of AI tools.

## Conclusion

Henney's central message transcends AI hype: technology is merely a mirror reflecting organizational culture and practices. To truly benefit from AI, companies must first address their fundamental inefficiencies, embrace sustainable pacing, and prioritize quality and understanding. Only then does AI become a genuine force multiplier for effective software development. Without this foundation, we simply speed up our journey down the wrong path.
