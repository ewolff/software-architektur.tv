## About the episode

Software architecture doesn’t live in diagrams – it’s forged in the
gaps between teams, technologies, timelines, and trade-offs. Today,
that landscape is shifting fast. LLMs and agentic workflows promise to
erase friction – but often amplify it through toolchain sprawl,
quality drift, and shifting accountability.

Yet friction is a signal. The most resilient teams channel it through
shared understanding, recognizing that we’re shaping socio-technical
systems – aligned through collaborative modelling woven into everyday
decisions across architects, developers, QA, analysts, etc.

This fishbowl session confronts the reality behind the hype: how
AI-generated code collides with architectural rigor, QA concerns, and
production constraints. We’ll examine classic tensions (Conway’s Law,
legacy vs. innovation, business urgency vs. technical debt) alongside
new ones (toolchain sprawl, quality drift, over-reliance on generative
AI).

As a conference attendee, you’re invited to step into the circle and
challenge assumptions. Whether you’re an architect, engineer, QA lead,
or system analyst, you’ll walk away with a refreshed perspective of
timeless insights – and the courage to question them when it matters
most.

This is a live stream from iSAQB Software Architecture Gathering.

## Summary

At the iSAQB Software Architecture Gathering, Lisa Maria Schäfer and
Lucas Dohmen moderated a fishbowl with Sam Newman, Jacqui Read, Dylan
Beattie, Gitte Klitgaard, Allen Holub and Adele Carpenter. Two seats on
stage were open for the audience, and the audience answered polls on
their phones. Lucas explained the title: friction is being removed in
projects, for example when a chatbot answers what you used to ask the
security team; some friction is good and some is bad.

### What Has Changed

Gitte sees a need for more engineering practices and more collaboration,
and observes the opposite: shortcuts, and people who work on their own,
so that the rest of the team finds new code in the morning and doesn't
know what it does. For Sam, the things we thought were good are still
good; AI is "like a magnifier" for poor communication, documentation and
architecture.

Dylan noted that AI is forcing institutions to write their decisions
down. Allen objected. In some shops there is a "huge leap back to
waterfall" with gigantic specifications, which almost guarantees that
the LLM builds the wrong thing. Dylan saw a difference: waterfall took
six months and $10 million to fail, and now "you can discover by tea
time that you got it completely wrong". Jacqui doubted that writing
things down helps if nobody understands the connections between the
information. Gitte wants to write down the right things, for example in
a decision log. Adele asked for the same hygiene for documentation as
for code.

In the poll, 74% said that software architecture has not fundamentally
changed in the last year, and 82% said that team dynamics have.

Avraham Poupko, from the audience, pointed out that much
of our shared understanding and thinking out loud happens in spoken
language, which an LLM has no access to. Allen finds working with LLMs
isolating for some people: "an LLM is not a person".

### What Has Stayed the Same

Small changes are still good, said Sam: the teams that do really well
with AI work in really small increments. Allen sees a paradox: the
companies that use AI most effectively already had solid engineering
practices. AI is an amplifier, "and it amplifies the bad really
effectively". Avraham called getting from imagination to a working
system a problem we will never solve, because with every new tool we
push ourselves harder. Jacqui expects more hype cycles; spec-driven
development reminds Jacqui of model-driven development.

Gitte asked what value all this provides: "what we really need is to
become slower". Adele: "Users just want their shit to work." Allen added
that an LLM makes competition easy, so companies have to compete by
solving real problems for real people.

### Agile Does Not Mean Fast

Jacqui would replace "agile" with "adaptable", because the business
hears "fast". Allen blamed Sutherland's "twice the work in half the
time": the only extra speed in an agile process comes from not building
what nobody wants. Sam explains agile as reducing the cost of making a
mistake and is not sure whether AI does that. Jacqui: "we're just
reducing the time it takes to spend the money and make the mistake". In
another poll, 70% said that the importance of soft skills has changed
due to AI. Gitte prefers to call them human skills.

### What Friction Means

For Adele, friction means being challenged on ideas by colleagues. Dylan
noted that friction has a negative connotation, but when it is useful we
call it traction: with zero friction, Formula One cars wouldn't move.
Adele: "You microdose the friction or you'll OD later." Allen doesn't
like this framing. In a psychologically safe environment people can
disagree and argue, and it's fine. Real friction for Allen is
organizational: in almost every company Allen deals with, management
prevents the company from doing what it needs to do to be successful.
Sam took up the racing analogy: sometimes you have to go slower to go
faster. The teams that got the most out of AI took the time to talk, to
experiment and to share ideas. Asked what friction is, the audience
named challenge, disagreement, conflict, learning, discussion and
change.

### More Friction, Less Friction

Sam wants more teams to see the value of slowing down to talk, and less
friction for experiments during the working day. Allen agreed that
learning is a normal work activity and should be paid, and told of a
startup where the whole company played cards after lunch: more work was
going on during those card games than in the rest of the week. For
Gitte, typing is a small part of development, but it is what looks like
work. Sam quoted a client who wants people "to spend more time being
more human". Gitte wants us to embrace our differences: at IBM, the team
made space for a developer who was different, because that difference
brought a lot of value.

Ingo Eichhorst, from the audience, reported that the friction in
generating code went basically to zero. Some of this person's teams now
add friction on purpose: every tenth pull request gets an AI-generated
quiz that has to be filled out. The friction to remove, in this view, is
the business process around an average ticket, which still takes four
days instead of five. Sam loved the idea of the quiz: "That's genius."
