## Summary

In this episode, Vaughn Vernon explains Ports and Adapters to Eberhard
Wolff. Vaughn considers the pattern simple and shows this with
diagrams. The talk goes on to patterns, frameworks and AI.

### Vaughn Vernon and the Books

Vaughn goes by the term software ecologist: someone who is concerned
with how we deal with software and who uses systems thinking for that.
Vaughn is writing a fifth book and edits a signature series at Pearson
Addison-Wesley. Eberhard recommends *Domain-Driven Design Distilled* as
an introduction. Vaughn is reviewing Susanne Kaiser's book on
Domain-Driven Design, Wardley Mapping and Team Topologies.

### Why Ports and Adapters?

Vaughn keeps reading that Hexagonal Architecture is complex and that
nobody needs it. Alistair Cockburn, who codified the pattern, asked
Vaughn on LinkedIn to call it Ports and Adapters. Vaughn thinks many
would not know what that means, though, and that this is why the title
of Cockburn's new book still says Hexagonal. In a certain region or
country in Europe, says Vaughn, people have made it a catch-all for
technologies such as Kafka, Docker and Kubernetes.

### From Layers to an Outside and an Inside

Vaughn starts with strict layers: user interface, application services,
domain model, infrastructure. One problem: the domain model often
declares an interface that the infrastructure has to implement, so the
infrastructure depends on a higher level. The dependency inversion
principle moves the infrastructure to the top, where it can depend on
interfaces declared below it.

Ports and Adapters primarily adds one idea: there is an outside and an
inside. An adapter on the driver side, such as a controller, takes an
HTTP request and adapts it to a well-designed interface on the inside,
the port. The port uses a repository interface. The implementation on
the outside is another adapter. Vaughn confesses an error: the red book
(*Implementing Domain-Driven Design*) points at the outside when it says
port. Vaughn says that this does not change the presentation of the
pattern.

A domain model on the inside is optional. If it exists, the port reads
an entity through the repository, tells it to do something and stores
it again. Eberhard sees easier testability as one of the biggest
benefits. Vaughn agrees: every part can be tested in isolation, for
example with an in-memory repository.

### Simple Tools for Simple Problems

Without a domain model, a transaction script may be the best choice: a
method starts a transaction, sends SQL statements to the database and
commits or rolls back. Vaughn takes the pattern from Martin Fowler's
*Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture*. Eberhard likes
that Vaughn recommends such an old pattern. Vaughn finds it sad that newer developers have
thrown patterns out and will have to reinvent them after mistakes.

### Many Adapters, the Same Ports

A larger diagram shows adapters for browser, mobile app, messaging and
command requests. They don't need four application services: they use
the same ports and the same domain model. On the other side sit, among
others, a projection adapter for CQRS, a Postgres repository, an
anti-corruption layer and a Kafka adapter. Nobody necessarily needs all
of them. It is the same simple pattern over and over again.

### Frameworks

Ein Realist writes in the chat that frameworks and libraries encapsulate
patterns, so developers might not recognize that they use one. Vaughn
says that Spring does not encapsulate Ports and Adapters, but enables it
in ways that programmers don't have to think about. In 2011 Vaughn told
the first DDD Summit that most teams probably use Hexagonal Architecture
without knowing it. To give a port its repository, it is enough to call
a constructor: "You're just using a constructor, right?" Vaughn is also
cautious about repository interfaces that follow a CRUD approach. A
repository in a framework does not necessarily follow the Domain-Driven
Design approach. And DDD does not require the repository pattern.

### AI: Plausible, but Not Necessarily True

Vaughn finds ChatGPT good at rewriting a paragraph. But when Vaughn
asked for an algorithm for risk and rate in car insurance, it looked
amazing until Joe Emison, CTO of an insurance company, replied that it
had nothing to do with anything. Vaughn also mentions lawyers who
submitted invented cases to a court. The danger: people use AI for
things where they cannot tell whether the answer is right.

Eberhard is impressed by text and code from ChatGPT, but sees it as
optimized to give the impression that it knows things, for example
through long answers. Eberhard also cites a study: with AI tools,
developers wrote less secure code and had more confidence in it. Vaughn
reports on Eric Evans's keynote at Explore DDD 2024: Evans is
researching LLMs and DDD almost full-time, is not betting on AI and
challenges the community to explore it.

## Bullet points

### Key Takeaways

- Vaughn Vernon considers Ports and Adapters simple: there is an outside and an inside, adapters on the outside, ports on the inside
- The name Hexagonal Architecture has stuck better, but Alistair Cockburn asked Vaughn to say Ports and Adapters and not to speak of layers
- Technology such as Kafka, Docker or Kubernetes is not what the pattern is about, says Vaughn
- Adapters translate requests, for example HTTP, into calls that suit a well-designed port; a port is an application service
- A repository implementation is an adapter too: the inside only knows the interface
- Vaughn's red book points at the wrong place for the port; Vaughn says the presentation of the pattern is still proper
- Every part can be tested in isolation, such as a port with an in-memory repository
- New kinds of adapters don't need their own ports: browser, mobile app and messaging use the same ports and domain model
- A domain model is optional; a transaction script can be the better choice for a simple problem
- Dependency injection can be a plain constructor call; Vaughn does not know why anyone really needs annotations for it
- A repository interface from a framework may follow a CRUD approach, and that is not the Domain-Driven Design approach; DDD also works without the repository pattern
- Vaughn warns against using AI where you cannot judge the answer; Eberhard Wolff sees ChatGPT as optimized to give the impression that it knows things
- Eberhard considers AI probably very useful on the code level, but you must not switch off your own brain
- Eric Evans is researching whether LLMs help with DDD, but is not betting on it, says Vaughn

### Glossary of Key Terms

- **Port**: Interface on the inside that takes adapted requests; in Vaughn's diagram, the ports are the application services
- **Adapter**: Part on the outside that translates between the outside world and a port, or that implements an interface of the inside
- **Driver side**: The side from which a user or another system drives the application to do something
- **Strict layers**: A layer may depend only on the layer directly below it; with relaxed layers, it may depend on any lower one
- **Dependency inversion principle**: Moves the infrastructure layer to the top, so that it depends on interfaces declared below it
- **Rich domain model**: Domain model with behavior, as opposed to an anemic model or a data model
- **Transaction script**: A method that starts a transaction, works on the database with simple SQL statements and commits or rolls back
- **Software ecologist**: Vaughn's term for someone concerned with how we deal with software, using systems thinking

- **Strategic Monoliths and Microservices**: Book from 2021; Vaughn is working on the follow-on *Implementing Strategic Monoliths and Microservices*
- **Domain-Driven Transformation**: Book by Carola Lilienthal and Henning Schwentner, about dealing with legacy systems
- **Pattern-Oriented Software Architecture, Vol. 1** (POSA 1): Source for strict and relaxed layers
- **Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture**: Book by Martin Fowler, describes transaction script
- **Spring**: Enables Ports and Adapters in ways that programmers don't have to think about, says Vaughn
- **ChatGPT**: Good at rewriting text; its answer on insurance risk and rate was a complete hallucination, says Vaughn
- **Claude** and **Mistral**: Vaughn has heard that Claude is way better, and reports that Eric Evans uses Mistral
- **RAG**: Suggested by Eric Evans, reports Vaughn
